## Failure analysis

### Trigram extraction

Trigram PMI on the filtered set (frequency at least 3) reaches precision@20 of 0.8 against the 40 trigram gold phrases, with a mean top-20 frequency of 3.45. The head of that list is real institutional trigrams (`vince lombardi trophy`, `president pro tem`, `maximum building height`, `law enforcement oversight`, `mile high stadium`). It also lets in chopped committee titles. `civil right utility` and `right utility economic` are not phrases anyone says. They are slices of the Seattle committee name "Civil Rights, Utilities, Economic Development" after lemmatization and after the comma was deleted. `national restaurant stock` is the same kind of damage around the National Western Stock Show. Trigram mining breaks down exactly where a transcript lists coordinated nouns: the window of three tokens is shorter than the name, so the scorer promotes the boundary pieces.

The unfiltered trigram PMI top-20 is entirely frequency 1 (fraction 1). With one attestation, position-specific marginals equal the joint count and trigram PMI saturates. Alphabetical tie-breaking then decides the list. That is not a ranking of domain phrases.

NLTK's trigram t-test and trigram likelihood ratio do not have this sparsity pattern, and they do not recover gold either. Trigram t precision@20 is 0. Its top row is `be go to`, the lemmatized form of "is going to" / "are going to". Trigram G^2 precision@20 is 0, and the whole top-20 is a paraphrase family of `thank you` plus the next word (`thank you mr`, `thank you madam`, `thank you councilmember`). G^2 is non-directional and scales with count, so the politeness formula crowds out every planning trigram. Three-word formulas that contain function words (`motion to approve`, `those in favor`, `in term of`) are real and are on the gold list, but they lose to either hapax exclusivity (PMI) or the thank-you family (G^2 and, for the t-test, the going-to family).

### A phrase found by only one metric

Among the eight from-scratch bigram rankings, `council member` (frequency 69) is inside only the log-likelihood top-20. It is a genuine role title and it is on the gold list. PMI does not rank it in the top 20 because too many rarer exclusive pairs score higher. Student's t does not rank it because `thank you` and `of the` have larger t statistics. Chi-square's top 20 is already full of near-perfect rare pairs whose statistic sits near N. Likelihood ratio is the metric that still has room, at frequency 69, for a high-count title whose expected count under independence is much smaller than 69. `cape verdean` (frequency 12) is the Yates-only top-20 item. It is a real demonym tied to the Cabo Verde hearings, and it is not on the gold list. Yates's continuity correction reorders the near-tie at the top of Pearson's chi-square enough to admit it and to drop some frequency-3 names. `this be` is a t-test-only top-20 item: lemmatization collapsed "this is" to a pseudo-collocation, and the t statistic then rewards the high count.

### Low counts, which dominate voice memos

On the unfiltered run, PMI's top-20 fraction at frequency 1 is 1, and the mean frequency of that top-20 is 1. Every one of those pairs is a hapax whose two words occur nowhere else, so PMI equals log2(N) and the alphabetical sort of the ties produces the list (`abstention iraqi`, `alma mater`, `astrobiologists democratization`, and so on). Some of those hapaxes are real names. Many are accidents of one sentence. The filtered run (frequency at least 3) is what keeps PMI usable: its filtered top-20 mean frequency is 3.9, precision@20 is 0.85, and the score-versus-frequency Spearman on the filtered set is -0.0705573. The negative sign is the low-frequency preference that remains even after the floor of 3.

Pearson's chi-square does the same thing when the filter is removed. A perfectly exclusive pair of any frequency, including 1, has chi-square equal to N (61728 here), so the unfiltered top is again an alphabetical hapax list (fraction at frequency 1: 1). That is the concrete sense in which chi-square does not penalize high-frequency noise the way a reader might hope, and also does not prefer high frequency at the very top: the statistic saturates. The textbook frequency effect shows up once pairs are not exclusive. `of the` is enormously common and only moderately dependent, so its chi-square is large in absolute terms but still below the saturated names. Yates changes the low-count story. Subtracting N/2 inside the absolute deviation demotes frequency-1 exclusive pairs. The unfiltered Yates top-20 has frequency-1 fraction 0 and is headed by the same mid-count names as the filtered list (`dry ice`, `boot camp`, `domestic violence`). The correction is doing the work the frequency filter does for Pearson.

Student's t does not need a hapax to go wrong. Filtered and unfiltered top-20s match, mean frequency 203, precision@20 0. Spearman of t against frequency on the filtered candidate set is 0.587808. The Manning and Schütze denominator sqrt(f(w1 w2)) is the Poisson variance-equals-mean assumption, and the numerator grows with the raw count whenever the pair beats independence by a modest ratio. Common grammatical bigrams therefore outrank rare domain terms. The normality assumption behind a t statistic is not met by these counts: bigram indicators are sparse and skewed, which is why a rank order driven by t should not be read as a calibrated test. Log-likelihood is more tolerant of sparse counts than Pearson's chi-square in Dunning's argument, and on this corpus it is the from-scratch metric that surfaces `council member`, `long beach`, and `mr president` inside the top 20. It still spends most of that top 20 on `thank you`, `of the`, and `you know` (precision@20 0.15, hits 3) because G^2 is non-directional and still increases with sample size. The phrase list and the check that the t formula matches Manning and Schütze and NLTK are in the section below. The reason chi-square and G^2 can share a high rank correlation and still disagree in that top 20 is the section after it.

PMI^2 and PMI^3 do not rescue the low-count problem. On the positive head of the PMI list, x^2 and x^3 are strictly increasing, so the filtered top-20 of PMI, PMI^2, and PMI^3 is the same 20 phrases (precision@20 0.85 for PMI^2). PMI^3 preserves the entire ranking of PMI (Spearman 1). PMI^2 does not: a negative PMI becomes a positive score. The most negative filtered pair, `the to`, has PMI about -4.6 at frequency 5 and a PMI^2 rank in the hundreds, not the top 20, because 4.6 squared is still far below a PMI of 14 squared. The sign flip is real and it would pollute a cutoff set on PMI^2 if the threshold were pushed down the list. It does not change the top 20 of this corpus.

### Student's t is a frequency-bias failure, and the formula is the documented one

The score in `src/models.py` is Manning and Schütze (1999) equation 5.10, t = (f(w1 w2) − f(w1) f(w2) / N) / sqrt(f(w1 w2)). N is the bigram-token count 61728, the same N the contingency table uses, not the word-token count 67046. On all 3864 filtered candidates, our t and NLTK `student_t` on the same cells have maximum absolute difference 0 and Spearman correlation 1. Direct substitution of the counts for `thank you` (f=488, f(w1)=524, f(w2)=1129, N=61728) returns 21.65687852. The same substitution for `of the` (f=396, f(w1)=1559, f(w2)=3155) returns 15.89554949. Both match our column and NLTK's column exactly.

The filtered top 20, with joint counts, is conversational filler and lemmatized copulas, not domain terms: `thank you` (488), `of the` (396), `want to` (210), `and i` (271), `i be` (306), `you know` (172), `in the` (256), `i think` (167), `we be` (234), `we have` (177), `that we` (180), `go to` (159), `do not` (140), `the city` (146), `so i` (136), `this be` (148), `there be` (114), `it be` (137), `i would` (93), `on the` (130). None is on the bigram gold list, so precision@20 is 0 (hits 0). The numerator grows with the raw excess count and the denominator only with the square root of the joint count, so these high-frequency pairs outrank rare names. `i be`, `we be`, `this be`, `there be`, and `it be` are that same bias after lemmatization collapsed "is" and "are" onto `be`.

### Chi-square and G^2 are asymptotically equivalent and still disagree at the top

Pearson's chi-square and Dunning's G^2 share a chi-square null distribution with 1 degree of freedom on a 2x2 table with fixed margins. That is a statement about the null as N grows. It does not make the two scores monotone transforms of each other on the strong associations that occupy a collocation ranking. Over the 3864 filtered candidates the Spearman correlation between our chi-square and our G^2 is 0.964364. The two top-20 lists share one phrase, `long beach`. Chi-square precision@20 is 0.95 with mean top-20 frequency 8.4. G^2 precision@20 is 0.15 with mean top-20 frequency 166.5.

The implementations are the standard statistics. Rebuilding every filtered 2x2 table and comparing with scipy: our Pearson value versus `chi2_contingency(table, correction=False)` has median absolute difference 8.88178e-16 and maximum 7.27596e-12 (Spearman 0.9999999999; the gap from 1 is one tie moved by that floating-point residue, and no table differs by more than 1e-8). Our Yates value versus `correction=True` has median absolute difference 4.44089e-16 and maximum 1.45519e-11 (Spearman 1). Our G^2 versus `chi2_contingency(table, correction=False, lambda_="log-likelihood")` has median and maximum absolute difference 0 (Spearman 1). Scipy's default `correction=True` applies Yates before the G statistic, so that call is not Dunning's G^2; against it our G^2 has median absolute difference 2.01502 and maximum 15.7377. On the same cells, NLTK `chi_sq` matches our Pearson (maximum absolute difference 7.27596e-12) and NLTK `likelihood_ratio` matches our G^2 (maximum 2.27374e-13). N in every table is the bigram count 61728. The four cells sum to that N. It is not the word-token count 67046.

The head splits for a concrete reason, and the vast O22 cell is not what inflates chi-square. An exclusive pair (O12 = O21 = 0, both marginals equal to the joint count) has Pearson chi-square equal to N at every frequency, because E11 = O11^2 / N is tiny and (O11 − E11)^2 / E11 saturates at N. Five phrases from the chi-square top 20, written as f(w1 w2), f(w1), f(w2), chi-square, G^2:

- `boot camp`: 4, 4, 4, 61728, 85.153329. Chi-square rank 1, G^2 rank 236. Of the Pearson sum, the O11 term is 61720.0003 and the O22 term is 0.0003. Of G^2, the O11 term 2·O11·ln(O11/E11) is 77.1536 (90.6%) and the O22 term is 7.9997. E11 is 0.000259.
- `coffee shop`: 3, 3, 3, 61728, 65.591138. G^2 rank 333. Same exclusive ceiling.
- `dry ice`: 8, 8, 9, 54868.4443, 152.936807. One stray count keeps it just under N.
- `mm hmm`: 6, 6, 7, 52908.8571, 117.122590.
- `san francisco`: 6, 7, 6, 52908.8571, 117.122590.

Eighty-five percent of that top 20 has a structural zero in O12 or O21. The list is low-count near-exclusive names (median frequency 5), not high-frequency grammar.

G^2 = 2 · Σ Oij ln(Oij / Eij) has no ceiling at N. Its largest piece on these tables is the O11 term, which scales with the joint count times the log lift, so frequent conversational pairs outrank exclusive names. Five phrases from the G^2 top 20, same columns:

- `thank you`: 488, 524, 1129, 24535.0907, 3890.292701. G^2 rank 1, chi-square rank 25 (0.397·N). E11 is 9.5839 and O11/E11 is 50.919. The O11 term is 3835.90 (98.6% of G^2). The O22 term is 960.63 and is partly cancelled by negative O12 and O21 terms. Pearson's O22 term is 3.81 (0.016% of chi-square).
- `want to`: 210, 228, 2311, 4958.1658, 1274.005384. Chi-square rank 143, G^2 rank 2.
- `you know`: 172, 996, 312, 5657.0308, 1025.240110. Chi-square rank 128, G^2 rank 3.
- `i think`: 167, 1625, 221, 4602.7152, 988.987220. Chi-square rank 154, G^2 rank 4.
- `do not`: 140, 527, 438, 5043.7588, 829.281463. Chi-square rank 142, G^2 rank 5.

So the 0.95 versus 0.15 precision@20 gap is a property of the two statistics on this corpus. Chi-square's top is whatever sits nearest the ceiling N, which here is medium-to-low frequency near-exclusive pairs. G^2's top is whatever maximizes the O11 log-lift term, which here is high-frequency filler. The bulk ranking still agrees (Spearman 0.964364). Nothing in the marginals, in O22, or in the log base was wrong.

### Gold-list incompleteness

The bigram gold list has 104 phrases and the trigram list has 40. Both were checked for frequency at least 3. Neither is exhaustive. Precision@20 divides by 20, so any true collocation that was not labeled counts as a miss and pulls precision down. That makes every precision@20 in the comparison table a lower bound, not an estimate of error against all true municipal collocations.

The bound is visible in the lists. `mm hmm` is a fair miss: it is a backchannel. `core mission`, `santa cruz`, `tara rise`, `cape verdean`, and `annual action plan` are fixed expressions that were not labeled. Counting them as junk would overstate the error of PMI, NPMI, and chi-square. `thank you` and `you know` are fixed discourse formulas and were deliberately left off the domain list; calling them junk would also be wrong, and calling them gold would have handed free hits to the t-test and to G^2. The qualitative labels keep those cases separate from grammatical junk (`of the`, `in the`) and from lemmatization junk (`this be`, `i be`, `be go to`).

Stopword removal is not free precision. Only 2 bigram gold phrases contain a stopword (`motion to`, `move to`), but the ablation changes the whole contingency table. After stopwords are deleted, Student's t precision@20 rises from 0 to 0.55 (11 hits) because `of the` can no longer occupy the top. PMI's precision stays high and does not need that deletion. The gain for t is real, and the cost is every formula that used a function word, including `in term of`.
